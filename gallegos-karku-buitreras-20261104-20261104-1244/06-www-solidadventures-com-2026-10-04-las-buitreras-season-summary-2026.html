Las Buitreras Season Summary 2026

Solid Adventures October 4, 2026 October 6th, 2026

Welcome to the Las Buitreras 2026 season summary!

As the curtain falls on another memorable season here at Las Buitreras, we look back on a year once again filled with good vibes and plenty of fish. It was a genuine pleasure to welcome a wonderful mix of old friends, newer friends, and first-time visitors—many of whom we’re certain will become future friends.

Although the season was undoubtedly a successful one, it certainly felt different from previous years. Without question, 2026 will be remembered as a season split into two very distinct halves.

During the first half of the season, daytime temperatures were considerably higher than the seasonal average. Combined with cooler-than-average nights, this created a rollercoaster effect on water temperatures. But that was only one of the challenges we faced. The other was the river level. A long, hot, dry summer brought an extended period of exceptionally low water in Patagonia, accompanied by crystal-clear conditions.

Watch some of the action from the season below

The second half of the season was almost the complete opposite—at least in terms of river levels. Daytime temperatures remained well above average, while increased cloud cover helped keep overnight temperatures relatively mild. Meanwhile, the river rose from exceptionally low to very high in a remarkably short period and, more or less, stayed there for the remainder of the season.

It certainly kept us on our toes!

But that’s exactly what fishing the Río Gallegos is all about. Challenge is part of its character.

Our regular guests know this all too well. They also know that if you’re prepared to embrace those challenges and adapt to the conditions, the rewards are there to be reaped.

This is not a river where a “rinse and repeat” approach works. Fish hold in different water depending on the conditions, meaning tactics constantly need to evolve. Fortunately, our experienced local guides know this river inside out.

The fish are always there… it’s simply a matter of unlocking the right window for one to jump through!

Throughout the season we continued to see excellent numbers of fish, particularly during the final two-thirds. More than 80% of all fish landed were chrome fish—an encouraging trend that has become increasingly noticeable over recent years.

Another very positive trend is the growing number of medium-sized fish, particularly those in the 6–9 lb range. Seeing more fish of this size can only be viewed as a good sign for the future. If these fish continue to thrive, they represent the next generation of the big sea trout that Las Buitreras has become famous for.

January

We started the season a little later than usual. Summer was in full bloom, with an abundance of flowers and wildlife, and new life could be seen in every direction. There are few places in the world that can match Patagonia at this time of year.

At the start of the season, the river sat right on the zero mark. The water was crystal clear—perhaps a little lower than we’d ideally like for this time of year—but perfect for fishing some of our favourite methods. Unfortunately, that situation didn’t last. As the days and weeks passed, the river continued to fall, dropping steadily from zero to -35 cm by the end of the month.

Light Spey rods and medium-weight single-hand rods equipped with floating lines and clear intermediate tips were not only the order of the day, but the order of the month… at least in most situations. We constantly adjusted leader length, tippet diameter, and fly size to suit the conditions, using a variety of proven patterns including tiny salmon doubles (sizes 12–16), bead-head nymphs (sizes 8–12), leeches, and, of course, the ever-faithful Sunray Shadow.

The opening week welcomed back a number of our most loyal guests, many of whom have been great friends of Solid Adventures for well over a decade—some for more than twenty years.

The first group travelled from Sweden and was hosted by Tomas Berggren. The second, led by Sten from Norway, included Keith, Derek, and Harry from the UK. Completing our opening line-up were first-time visitors Antti and Iiro from Finland.

The remaining two weeks of January were hosted by our good friend John Bilotta from the USA. John’s guests were a great mix of returning visitors and newcomers, including Marc and Isabelle, and Jonathan and Catherine.

January certainly presented its challenges. Prolonged spells of hot weather, with daytime highs reaching 32°C, combined with bright skies and very little cloud cover, had a significant impact on both the river and, to a degree, the quality of the fishing.

Water temperatures were in a constant cycle of rising and falling, never really settling into any period of stability. To make matters even more unusual, the moderate to strong westerly winds that we normally rely on simply failed to appear.

Guests were encouraged to keep noise to an absolute minimum while approaching the river and to pay close attention to how much disturbance they created while wading. On warm, calm days, noisy footsteps or excessive false casting are easily noticed by the fish.

Río Gallegos is one of those rare rivers capable of producing fish in almost any conditions, and adapting to whatever nature throws at us is a huge part of the attraction here at Las Buitreras. We’ve been meeting these challenges for more than twenty-five years and know that, with the right tactics and mindset, success is never far away.

Despite the difficult conditions, our guests still enjoyed some excellent fishing. Most anglers landed healthy numbers of fish, with many reaching double figures. There were simply too many memorable captures to mention everyone individually, but a few certainly deserve a special mention.

  • Tomas Berggren – 14 lb
  • Sten Ruud – 14 lb
  • Marc Dutil – 15.5 lb
  • John Bilotta – 15.5 lb
  • Tom Randgaard – 14.5 lb

Fish of the month, however, was never really in doubt. This incredible fish outweighed every other contender by more than 5 lb!

A huge congratulations goes to our long-time friend and annual visitor Jamie McLelland, who tempted a magnificent chrome-bright 21 lb sea trout from the Zone 2 favourite Cogedero using an old-school Girdle Bug.

Well done, Jamie… congratulations on adding yet another twenty-pounder to your ever-growing hit list!

Biggest fish: Jamie McLelland 21lbs

Average size: 7.6lbs

Best patterns: Copper Johns, Marianne, Salmon Doubles, Sunray           Shadow.

February 

By the start of February, the heat had begun to ease and daytime temperatures returned to something much closer to the seasonal average (around 18–21°C). Light levels, however, remained fairly high, particularly during the first half of the month.

The wind, on the other hand, continued to keep us guessing. This season the prevailing direction appeared to shift more towards the northwest, rather than the west and southwest winds we have become so accustomed to over the years. Not only had the prevailing direction changed, but those winds also became far less consistent. Almost every week we experienced prolonged periods when the wind blew from several different directions—sometimes all within the same day. On other occasions it disappeared altogether, leaving us with completely calm conditions.

River levels remained very low.

We began the month at -35 cm, and although the river continued to fall over the following weeks, it did so very gradually, never dropping below -45 cm. By the end of the month we finally began to see small pushes of fresh water entering the system.

Slowly but steadily, the river began to recover. Initially the rise was only around 20 cm, but it was enough to freshen things up and maintain consistent fishing. This pattern continued over the following weeks, bringing the river some much-needed stability and providing almost perfect conditions for fishing our favourite methods.

Our daily schedule consists of two fishing sessions—one in the morning and one in the evening—with lunch and a well-earned siesta in between. This allows both the river and our guests to rest while avoiding the hottest part of the day and making the most of the prime fishing hours.

Here at Las Buitreras we are fortunate to enjoy private access to more than 40 km of double-bank fishing, with over 50 named pools. Whatever conditions nature decides to throw at us, we almost always have options. Lower water may make a handful of pools difficult to fish, but it also concentrates the sea trout into the deeper holding water and well-oxygenated runs, often making them easier to locate and target.

River conditions across much of Southern Patagonia were far from ideal during the first half of the season, and many rivers experienced lower catch rates.

February, however, was a very different story here at Las Buitreras.

Healthy numbers of fish continued to enter the system, and in many pools they regularly announced their arrival by crashing through the surface, leaving little doubt they were there.

Our tactics remained largely unchanged from January. Light Spey rods (7/8 wt) and single-hand rods (7/8 wt), paired with floating lines or clear intermediate tips, continued to dominate. Long tapered leaders finished with small to medium-sized nymphs or salmon doubles proved particularly effective during daylight hours, while shorter leaders armed with Sunray Shadows or leeches came into their own during the Golden Hour.

There were countless memorable moments throughout February—far too many to mention everyone—but a few stand out.

We began the month by welcoming a truly international group, with guests from no fewer than six different countries.

Among them was our good friend Horst Rathgeber—better known to us simply as The Comedian—from Germany. During one evening session, his guide selected a Sunray Shadow to fish one of our standout pools of the season, Old Bridge, during the Golden Hour. It proved to be exactly the right choice. On what would be his final cast of the evening, and in almost complete darkness, Horst hooked and landed a magnificent fresh fish weighing 16.5 lb.

During the same week, Tomas and Petra also enjoyed excellent sport. Their trip was highlighted by a beautiful fresh fish of just over 15 lb for Petra, again from Old Bridge, this time taken in broad daylight on a medium-sized Girdle Bug.

Throughout the remainder of February we welcomed hosted groups from Quebec, Germany, and another wonderfully international group made up of guests from seven different countries.

The fishing was excellent throughout, with several weeks producing outstanding numbers.

Among our returning groups was a small party from Sweden, led by our good friend Pontus. One member of his group, Michael Dahlberg, received a rather unforgettable introduction to Río Gallegos.

Michael was relatively new to Spey casting and preferred, for the most part, to fish with a single-hand rod. During his very first session he was rotated onto Zone 1, home to one of our most iconic pools, Kitchen.

Fishing his trusty 7 wt single-hander with a tiny bead-head Copper John, Michael was met with an act of extreme violence as a magnificent 17 lb sea trout smashed his tiny swung fly. After a long—and at times rather painful—battle, he eventually guided the fish safely into the net.

Michael went on to enjoy an exceptional week, landing impressive numbers of fish, including another beautiful double of 15 lb from another of the season’s standout pools, Old Bridge.

On the very same day, his good friend and host Pontus added a superb 16 lb fish from Peca, another Zone 1 favourite.

Other notable catches during the month included Marcel Schneider, who landed fish of 15 lb and 14 lb on consecutive casts from Kitchen—a feat that will take some beating.

John Redding enjoyed a memorable week that included a remarkable hat-trick of fish weighing 15, 14 and 12 lb.

Keith Wight also experienced outstanding fishing, landing several double-figure fish, the best reaching 15 lb.

Another familiar face was Maros from Iceland, well known to many through social media. A highly accomplished sea trout angler in his own right, Maros quickly put his experience to good use, landing an impressive number of quality fish.

His best was a stunning fresh blue female weighing 18 lb from the Zone 5 favourite Barranca Negra, taken on a size 10 bead-head Copper John. Not content with that, he soon followed it with another superb fresh fish of 15 lb.

Our old friend Andrea Fioretti from Italy, who visits us every season and usually stays for more than a week, also enjoyed another highly successful trip.

His best fish tipped the scales at just over 16 lb, backed up by several more impressive doubles, including a couple weighing 14 lb.

The biggest fish of the month belonged to another of our annual visitors and dear friend, Sven, from Germany.

While fishing the Zone 5 pool Barranca Blanca, Sven tempted a very angry, slightly coloured sea trout with a medium-sized Girdle Bug. The result was a magnificent fish weighing 19.5 lb (95 × 52 cm).

Not quite a twenty… but who cares?
What a magnificent fish, and a capture that will live long in the memory.
Congratulations, Sven!

March / April

March marks the beginning of autumn here in the Deep South, and with it usually comes a change in tactics. This season, however, “change” became the theme of almost every day on the river.

The weather remained as unpredictable as ever—no great surprise for this time of year. Air temperatures started the period close to the seasonal average, ranging between 15–20°C, but as the weeks passed there was very little cooling. We finished the season well above the long-term average, with daytime temperatures still reaching 11–17°C.

Normally, March tells a very different story. By the end of the month, overnight frosts are common and it’s not unusual to wake to ice along the riverbank four or five mornings each week. This season was different. The warmer temperatures meant that any snowfall in the Andes melted quickly, sending regular pulses of icy water into the system. The result was rapidly changing river levels and fluctuating water temperatures.

The wind continued to keep everyone guessing. While calmer conditions are fairly typical in autumn, this year we experienced constant changes in both direction and strength. Our guides were continually adapting their daily plans, often changing tactics several times in a single session to stay one step ahead of the conditions.

Light levels remained much the same as they had throughout the season. Cloud cover was often limited, leaving bright skies overhead. At this time of year, however, that’s no bad thing. The sunshine helps maintain water temperatures while improving visibility in what was often a high, coloured river.

River conditions during the latter part of the season proved more complicated than we would have liked. We began March with the river sitting around -40 cm, having benefited from several small but regular rises of between 5 and 20 cm during the previous weeks. These fresh pushes had brought welcome stability to both water levels and temperatures.

Everything changed during the second week of March with a substantial rise from the Andes, lifting the river to approximately 20 cm above the zero mark. After spending much of the season battling low water, the extra flow was certainly welcome.

From that point onwards, the river continued to receive regular fresh pushes. Some were modest, while others were substantial. On one occasion the river rose by well over a metre—too much to obtain an accurate level reading.

Despite these dramatic fluctuations, we never lost a single day’s fishing.

There were undoubtedly periods when high, coloured water made life more difficult, but we simply adapted. Every rise demanded a different approach, and every drop opened up new opportunities.

At this point we’d like to say thank you to our guiding team.

Between the six of them, they possess well over a century of experience on the Río Gallegos. Most were introduced to the river by their fathers or older brothers, and that lifetime of local knowledge is impossible to replace. Their ability to read changing conditions and make the right decisions, often at a moment’s notice, is one of the greatest strengths of Las Buitreras.

Our tackle and tactics evolved constantly throughout this period.

We began March fishing our standard floating Scandi heads with floating or intermediate tips—an approach that serves us exceptionally well through most of the season.

As river levels climbed, we even went for Skagit heads—equipment that spends 99.99% of its life tucked safely away at Las Buitreras as this shallow river is best fished with lighter lines, such as Scandi heads.

When conditions demanded it, we paired them with tungsten tips ranging from T8 to T14, allowing us to slow our flies down and reach fish holding deep in the heavier flows.

As river levels eased, we happily returned to our preferred Scandi setups, fishing sink tips from S3 through to S7. At other times we switched back to intermediate tips, stripping long, slim Sunray Shadows just beneath the surface—often a deadly tactic when deeper presentations failed to produce.

Fly choice became less about pattern and far more about profile and size.

With a small tear in our eyes, we packed away the bead-head nymphs and tiny salmon doubles that had served us so well earlier in the season. Their place was taken by larger, brighter patterns. Woolly Buggers and streamers in sizes 2–6 became our daily workhorses, with Yellow Yummy and Poison leading the charge. Sunray Shadows and various Intruder-style flies also enjoyed plenty of success whenever conditions suited.

Our guests enjoyed some outstanding fishing throughout March and into April.

There were certainly days when high, coloured water made us work harder for every fish, but they were quickly forgotten when conditions settled. From the beginning of March onwards, weekly catches regularly exceeded 100 fish, with several weeks producing well over 120.

Many anglers arrive believing that Río Gallegos is an extremely technical river. Although the fact that is more shallow We’re never quite sure where that reputation comes from.

Yes, the river demands the right approach and offers more variation than other well known seatrout rivers in Patagonia—but that’s exactly why you have a guide on your side.

Considering the conditions we encountered this season, we couldn’t be happier with the results. The early weeks tested us with low water, high temperatures and crystal-clear conditions. The latter part of the season brought repeated rises, coloured water and constantly changing tactics.

Yet throughout it all, the river continued to produce.

One standout newcomer this year was American guest Anthony Gaeta.

This was Anthony’s first experience not only with a Spey rod, but with fly fishing altogether.

His progress throughout the week was remarkable. During his very first session he landed two fish, including one weighing 10 lb. By the end of the week he had added another ten to the tally—an impressive achievement by any standard.

  • Marco Landi – 19 lb from Ochenta

Congratulations, Pascal!
