Fishing Licenses in Argentina

Every angler who fishes in Argentina needs a licence — but the system is simpler than it looks, and if you fish with a lodge or guide it is usually taken care of for you. Here is exactly how licensing works across Patagonia and the other great fishing regions, what it costs, where to buy one, and the handful of rules a visiting fly fisher genuinely needs to know before stepping into the river.

How fishing licences work in Argentina

Argentina does not have a single national fishing licence. Instead, licences are issued by provincial governments and by the Administración de Parques Nacionales (APN — the National Parks Administration), and the good news for the travelling angler is that the main Patagonian jurisdictions have unified their system. A single standard licence issued by any one of the provinces of Neuquén, Río Negro or Chubut, or by the National Parks Administration, is valid for fishing across all of those jurisdictions — the whole heart of trout Patagonia, from Bariloche and Junín de los Andes to Esquel and the national parks in between.

Three other famous regions — Tierra del Fuego, Santa Cruz and Corrientes — run their own separate systems, each with its own permit, price and season. We cover each of them below. In every case, if you’re booking a guided trip the operator arranges the correct licence for you.

The unified Patagonian licence: prices

The unified licence (Neuquén, Río Negro, Chubut and National Parks) comes in daily, 10-day and seasonal versions, with a cheaper scale for Argentine residents and a surcharge if you want to troll (spin-fishing from a moving boat). The figures below are the official 2025/26 season tariffs in Argentine pesos, with an approximate US-dollar equivalent for planning.

Either way, a licence is a small line in a fishing trip’s budget, and your guide will have the current number.

A few useful details from the regulation:

  • Residents get large discounts, and there are reduced or free categories: resident minors aged 13–17 pay a lower seasonal rate, while retired senior citizens, pensioners, children under 13 and disabled persons (with ID) fish free of charge.
  • A seasonal licence covers the whole official season — for 2025/26 that runs 1 November 2025 to 1 May 2026 (dates vary slightly by zone).
  • If you want to troll from a boat, you need an additional trolling permit on top of the standard licence: roughly AR$20,000 daily (~US$13), AR$40,000 for 10 days (~US$27), or AR$80,000 seasonal (~US$53). Fly and spin fishing from the bank or a drifting boat do not need it.

Fishing outside unified Patagonia: the other three regions

If your trip is to the sea-trout rivers of Tierra del Fuego, the king-salmon rivers of Santa Cruz, or the dorado marshes of Corrientes, you are outside the unified Patagonian system and need that region’s own permit. Here is how each works.

Tierra del Fuego — the sea-trout province

The far-south island province, home of the legendary Río Grande and its record sea-run brown trout, issues its own licence through the provincial environment authority (Ministerio de Producción y Ambiente). The 2025/26 season runs 1 November 2025 to 1 May 2026 (Resolution 276/25). Non-resident rates are higher than mainland Patagonia, and there is an extra fee to fish the eight designated “preference zones” — which include the prime sea-trout beats — so the world-class water carries a premium. In practice, the Río Grande lodges bundle the licence and zone permit into their booked weeks, so guests rarely handle it directly. Non-resident prices for 2025/26: daily AR$60,000 (~US$40), three days AR$90,000 (~US$60), weekly AR$150,000 (~US$100), full season AR$200,000 (~US$135), plus the preference-zone surcharge.

Santa Cruz — the king-salmon province

Santa Cruz, the deep-south province of the Río Santa Cruz and its giant king salmon, runs its permits through the Consejo Agrario Provincial, and — usefully — sells them entirely online via the provincial SIPESQ portal (pesca.santacruz.gob.ar). Permits must be requested at least three days in advance for review, so don’t leave it to the last minute if you’re travelling independently. The season runs from 1 October through the austral summer. Non-resident prices for 2025/26: daily AR$60,000 (~US$40), weekly AR$200,000 (~US$135), full season AR$300,000 (~US$200). Residents pay far less (a full season is just AR$25,000). As with the other salmon rivers, the estancias and outfitters normally arrange it for their guests.

Corrientes & the Iberá — the dorado marshes

The subtropical north-east is a different world: warm-water fishing for golden dorado in the Paraná, Corriente and the vast Iberá wetlands, licensed by the province of Corrientes (Dirección de Recursos Naturales). The defining feature here is not the price but the closed season (veda): to protect spawning dorado, the province closes or restricts fishing for part of the summer — for 2025/26 a veda was set in late October 2025 and lifted in early March 2026 (Resolution 34/2026). Corrientes has also pushed hard toward total catch-and-release (“pesca con devolución”), which is the norm for dorado. Because seasons, veda dates and zones shift year to year and the fishing is almost always done from boats with a guide or lodge, this is very much a region where you let the outfitter handle the permit and tell you when the water is open. See our golden dorado guide for the fishery itself.

Rule of thumb: one unified licence covers all of trout Patagonia (Bariloche, San Martín, Esquel, the national parks). Tierra del Fuego, Santa Cruz and Corrientes each need their own — but in all three, the lodges that run those fisheries arrange it for you as part of the trip.

Where and how to buy your licence

There are three practical ways to get a fishing licence in Argentina:

1. Through your lodge or guide (easiest)

By far the most common route for visiting anglers. Book a guided day or a lodge week and the licence is almost always organised for you — either included or added to your bill — along with a briefing on the local regulations. If you are fishing guided, you generally don’t need to do anything yourself.

2. Online, in advance

The Patagonian provinces, the National Parks Administration, and provinces like Santa Cruz (through its SIPESQ portal) sell licences on their official websites, so you can arrive already covered. This is the tidy option for independent anglers — find the official permit portal for the province or park you’ll fish, pay online, and carry the digital or printed permit with you.

3. In person at a fishing shop (casa de pesca)

Every fishing town in Patagonia — Bariloche, Junín de los Andes, San Martín de los Andes, Esquel — has tackle shops (casas de pesca) that sell licences over the counter, and staff there are an excellent source of current, local advice. Bring your passport. Provincial tourist offices and some park entrances also issue them.

The rules every visiting angler should know

Buying the licence is the easy part; fishing within the rules is what protects these fisheries. The full regulation is detailed and changes zone by zone, but a handful of principles apply almost everywhere:

  • Daylight hours only. Fishing is permitted during defined daytime windows, which shift by month — no night fishing.
  • Single, barbless hooks are required in the many catch-and-release waters (pinch your barbs down).
  • Catch-and-release is mandatory on a large and growing number of rivers and stretches — and for certain species everywhere (brown trout on the Limay, dorado in Corrientes). Where a limited harvest is allowed, strict size and bag limits apply.
  • No felt-soled wading boots. Felt soles are banned across Patagonia to stop the spread of the invasive alga didymo (“rock snot”). Use rubber-soled boots.
  • Disinfect your gear. Waders, boots and nets must be dried and disinfected between waters; foreign anglers are required to fish with new or properly disinfected equipment.
  • Native species (such as perca and the native galaxiids) have their own protections — release is required in many places. Exotic salmonids, carp and silverside are the fish generally targeted in Patagonia.
How the regulation is structured: the official Patagonian rulebook has a general first part (rules that apply everywhere) followed by annexes for each province and national park, where specific rivers, zones, dates and exceptions are listed. That’s why a river can be fly-only and catch-and-release in one stretch and allow a rainbow or two in another — always check the annex for the specific water you’re fishing. This is exactly the kind of local detail a guide handles for you.

The season

The general Patagonian trout season runs roughly from 1 November to the end of April/early May, corresponding to the austral spring, summer and autumn. Santa Cruz opens a little earlier (1 October) and Tierra del Fuego runs 1 November to 1 May; Corrientes dorado fishing is shaped instead by its summer spawning veda. Exact opening and closing dates — and special early or extended seasons on particular waters — are set each year, so confirm them for your dates. Frequently asked questions

Do I really need a fishing licence in Argentina?

Yes. A licence is legally required to fish anywhere in Argentina, and wardens do check. If you fish with a lodge or guide it is normally arranged for you, but you must always have a valid licence for the jurisdiction you’re fishing.

Does one licence cover all of Patagonia?

Almost. A single standard licence from Neuquén, Río Negro, Chubut or the National Parks Administration is valid across all four of those jurisdictions — the core of trout Patagonia. Tierra del Fuego, Santa Cruz and Corrientes run separate systems, but your outfitter arranges those.

How much does a fishing licence cost for a foreigner?

On the 2025/26 reference tariff, a non-resident paid about AR$50,000 (~US$33) per day in unified Patagonia, AR$140,000 (~US$95) for 10 days, or AR$280,000 (~US$185) for the full season. Tierra del Fuego and Santa Cruz cost a little more. Peso values and the exchange rate change constantly, so confirm the current figures — but it’s a small part of a fishing trip’s overall cost.

How do the sea-trout and king-salmon provinces differ?

Tierra del Fuego (Río Grande sea trout) runs 1 Nov–1 May with a surcharge to fish its prime “preference zones”; Santa Cruz (king salmon) opens 1 October and sells permits online through its SIPESQ portal, requiring three days’ notice. Both cost a bit more than mainland Patagonia for non-residents, and both are normally arranged by the lodges.

When can I fish for golden dorado in Corrientes?

Dorado fishing in Corrientes and the Iberá is governed by a spawning closed season (veda) over part of the summer — for 2025/26 it ran from late October 2025 to early March 2026. Catch-and-release is the norm. Dates shift each year, so fish it through a lodge or guide who tracks the current regulation.

Can I buy a fishing licence online before I arrive?

Yes. The Patagonian provinces, the National Parks Administration and Santa Cruz (via SIPESQ) sell licences through official websites, so you can arrive already covered. You can also buy one in person at any fishing shop (casa de pesca) in the fishing towns — bring your passport.

Are felt-soled wading boots really banned?

Yes, across Patagonia, to prevent the spread of the invasive alga didymo. Bring rubber-soled boots, and expect to disinfect your gear between waters — foreign anglers are specifically required to fish with new or disinfected equipment.
