Key facts
Species fished in Patagonia: rainbow trout (Oncorhynchus mykiss), brown trout (Salmo trutta) and brook trout (Salvelinus fontinalis).
Sea-run brown trout (Río Grande, Tierra del Fuego): an average of about 4.5 kg (10 lb), with fish of 9 to 13.5 kg (20 to 30 lb) every year, according to Todd Moen (Catch Magazine).
Temperature: trout are most active between 7 and 15.5 °C; above 20 °C mortality after release spikes.
Rules: many Patagonian waters are fly-fishing only, with barbless hooks and mandatory release.
The trout of Patagonian fishing
Fly fishing in Patagonia revolves around three species: rainbow and brown trout, the most sought after, and brook trout (fontinalis), which today is found only in the headwaters of the Negro, Limay and Chubut rivers, according to a 2014 study. Mountain rivers, spring creeks, lakes and the Atlantic coast of Tierra del Fuego offer very different fishing for the same species.
When and how they arrived
None of the three is native. The first official introduction of salmonids in Patagonia was in 1904, and two hatcheries were built to support it: the Nahuel Huapi hatchery, on the Limay River (1904 to 1931), and another on the Santa Cruz River (1904 to 1913). An analysis of the stocking between 1904 and 2011 across 54 drainages (Macchi and Vigliano, Ecología Austral, 2014) found that rainbow trout was stocked in 96.4% of the basins and sub-basins, brook trout in 72.7% and brown trout in 67.3%.
Brook trout is the one that has lost the most ground: it is recorded in 17 basins, 42.5% of those where it was introduced. Until the 1940s it was in 100% of the Negro, Limay and Chubut river basins; today it is found only in their headwaters. The authors attribute that decline, apparently, to the increase in rainbow and brown trout stocking.
Rainbow trout in Patagonia
It is the most stocked species: it reached 96.4% of Patagonia’s basins and sub-basins, according to the 2014 analysis by Macchi and Vigliano. It is fished during each province’s season, which opens in November and closes between April and May, with exceptions by water.
Brown trout in Patagonia
It was stocked in 67.3% of the basins and sub-basins. On the Río Grande, in Tierra del Fuego, there is a form that migrates to the sea and comes back at huge sizes: it is described below. On any water, check the rules in the province’s annex.
Brook trout (fontinalis) in Patagonia
It was stocked in 72.7% of the basins, but today it is recorded in 42.5% of those where it was introduced, and in northern Patagonia only in the headwaters of the Negro, Limay and Chubut rivers. Ask your guide which water is worth trying.
How big Patagonian trout get
The most complete measured data we read come from a doctoral thesis at the University of Buenos Aires (Baigún, 2001, Ecología pesquera de lagos y embalses patagónicos, tables 4.1.4 to 4.1.6), based on experimental fishing with batteries of gill nets, once per water in summer, between 1984 and 1987. They are net samples from those lakes and reservoirs, not anglers’ catches; they give an order of magnitude:
Rainbow trout: mean length per water from 25.5 to 54.3 cm and mean weight from 0.14 to 2.2 kg; the largest fish in the nets measured 78.6 cm and weighed 6.1 kg.
Brook trout (fontinalis): mean length from 22.3 to 47.5 cm and mean weight from 0.15 to 1.96 kg; the largest, 51.5 cm and 2.8 kg.
Brown trout (seven waters; Mascardi appears twice): mean length from 43.6 to 53.4 cm and mean weight from 0.76 to 1.7 kg; the largest, 66.5 cm and 2.45 kg. In the brown trout table the mean-weight column is headed “(mm)”; from the values we read it as grams.
Sea-run brown trout. A thesis from the Universidad Nacional del Comahue (Giese, Centro Regional Universitario Bariloche, Estructuración geográfica en poblaciones de trucha marrón (Salmo trutta L.) migratoria en ríos de la Patagonia Austral) compares the adult populations of the Gallegos, Grande and Irigoyen rivers. According to its abstract, the length of adults, taking marine age and smolt year into account, was greater in the Gallegos than in the Fuegian rivers (Grande and Irigoyen), and no weight differences were observed among the three rivers. According to Giese’s doctoral thesis (Universidad Nacional del Comahue, “Estructuración geográfica en poblaciones de trucha marrón migratoria en ríos de la Patagonia Austral”), in the adults sampled for the study the mean length was 66.5 cm and the mean weight 3.8 kg in the Gallegos River; 62.8 cm and 4.0 kg in the Grande; and 57.5 cm and 3.2 kg in the Irigoyen.
What a regulation portal says. According to reglamentodepesca.org.ar (page “Peces de la Patagonia”; the site does not name its publisher), the brown trout “reaches the largest sizes (e.g. 16.3 kg in Lake Nahuel Huapi)” and in the Río Grande, Tierra del Fuego, “7 world records have been homologated” for the variety that migrates to the sea; of the brook trout it says that “in more productive waters, sizes above 4 kg are frequent” (our translation of the Spanish original).
IGFA all-tackle records, shown apart: they are not usual sizes. Rainbow: 21.77 kg (48 lb), Sean Konrad, Lake Diefenbaker (Canada), 5/9/2009. Brown: 20.10 kg (44 lb 5 oz), Seumas Petrie, Ohau Canal, Twizel (New Zealand), 27/10/2020. Brook: 6.57 kg (14 lb 8 oz), Dr. J. W. Cook, Nipigon River (Ontario, Canada), 21/7/1915. None is from Patagonia (IGFA, read 13/11/2026). The IGFA lists the brown trout record under a single name, “Trout, brown (Salmo trutta)”, which includes “sea trout” among its alternative names, with no separate category for the sea form.

The sea-run brown trout of the Río Grande
On the Río Grande, in Tierra del Fuego, the introduced brown trout developed the habit of migrating to the Atlantic Ocean to feed, because the river holds little forage, and they come back at huge sizes. According to Todd Moen, in the Catch Magazine video
, the average is around 10 lb (about 4.5 kg) and fish of 20 to 30 lb (9 to 13.5 kg) are recorded every year. The run enters from November and continues until April.
What temperature they like
According to a fly-fishing video by Ventures Fly Co
, trout are most active between 7 and 15.5 °C and hardly feed below 3.3 °C; above 20 °C catch-and-release mortality spikes from lack of oxygen and it is best to stop fishing. It is not specific to Patagonia, but it helps you decide the time of day and the month.
Rules for fishing them
Patagonian fishing is governed by a common continental regulation, with annexes by province: each water has its own opening and closing dates, and many are fly-fishing only, with barbless hooks and mandatory release. You need the season permit. Confirm with your guide or the provincial authority; our guide to San Martín and Junín de los Andes
details it for Neuquén.

Frequently asked questions
What kinds of trout are there in Patagonia?
Three: rainbow (Oncorhynchus mykiss), brown (Salmo trutta) and brook trout or fontinalis (Salvelinus fontinalis). None is native: the first official introduction of salmonids in Patagonia was in 1904 (Macchi and Vigliano, Ecología Austral, 2014). Brown trout has, on the Río Grande in Tierra del Fuego, a form that migrates to the sea and comes back at huge sizes.
Where can you catch brook trout in Patagonia?
Today it is recorded in 42.5% of the basins where it was introduced and, in northern Patagonia, only in the headwaters of the Negro, Limay and Chubut rivers (Macchi and Vigliano, 2014). Ask your guide which water is worth trying; each water has its own rules.
How much do Patagonian trout weigh?
In Baigún’s experimental gill-net fishing (2001, UBA thesis; summers 1984 to 1987), mean weights per water were 0.14 to 2.2 kg for rainbow, 0.15 to 1.96 kg for brook and 0.76 to 1.7 kg for brown trout; the largest fish in those nets weighed 6.1 kg (rainbow), 2.8 kg (brook) and 2.45 kg (brown). They are net samples, not anglers’ catches. According to reglamentodepesca.org.ar, the brown trout reaches the largest sizes (16.3 kg in Lake Nahuel Huapi) and the brook trout exceeds 4 kg in productive waters. The IGFA records (21.77 kg rainbow, 20.10 kg brown, 6.57 kg brook) come from Canada and New Zealand.
